Around 1880 the author of *Anna Karenina* stopped writing fiction, taught himself enough Greek to re-translate the New Testament, and produced a work most readers have never opened: *The Union and Translation of the Four Gospels*. This dive is about that work as an event in a life — why he did it, what it meant for his family, and how it reached English. (Two earlier dives cover the rest: [what he did to the text](../../../../docs/research/themes/fire-metaphor/index.html), and [where the text lives](../../../../docs/research/_meta/jubilee-edition-tei-corpus/index.html).)

He framed it himself as the third part of a four-part project: *A Confession*, then a demolition of church dogma, then the translation-and-harmony, then *What I Believe*. The order matters — the harmony is the product of his conversion, not its cause. He came to it, by his own account, "at fifty years of age," in despair, having wanted to kill himself. He read the Greek with lexicons, distrusted the existing translations because they post-dated the church's fixing of the meaning, and combined the four Gospels not by chronology, as earlier harmonists had, but by what he took to be the single meaning of the teaching — a structure of twelve chapters he later claimed to find mirrored in the Lord's Prayer. Two decades on he admitted the philology was often forced: "artificial and probably incorrect."

The work consumed him and produced nothing the household could use. To his friend Strakhov: "I work very much and passionately, though I write nothing… work for myself, which I shall never print." His wife's diaries record the other side of it. Sofia Andreevna watched him grow "listless, silent and absorbed — he keeps reading," felt herself "a working machine," and on 26 August 1882 wrote the entry that names the strain outright: he had cried aloud "that his most passionate thought is to leave the family… He has become imbued with Christianity… I am jealous of it." She also called the endless, unprintable work "God's will" — which is why the primary record is more ambivalent than the Sofia-as-victim, Chertkov-as-villain story of the films and popular biographies.

Because it could not be published in Russia, the harmony first appeared in Geneva (1892–94) and was not legally printed at home until the Soviet Jubilee Edition — Tom 24, 1957. Its path into English is the surprise the reader's own copy preserves: *The Four Gospels Harmonised and Translated* (Brotherhood Publishing Co. / Walter Scott, 1895–96) was made "at the request of the author" from the faulty Geneva text by a translator named nowhere in the book — and carries, in facsimile at the front, Tolstoy's own handwritten certificate that the rendering "has been made by a competent translator and with my consent." Only two of the projected three parts ever appeared; the one complete English harmony is Leo Wiener's, of 1904.
