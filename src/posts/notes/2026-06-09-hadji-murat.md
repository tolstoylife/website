Between 1896 and 1904 Tolstoy wrote his last work of fiction — *Хаджи-Мурат*, **Hadji Murat** — and never published it. He wrote it in secret, called it self-indulgence, and set it aside; it appeared only in 1912, two years after his death. This corpus dive reads the novella from its own text in the Jubilee Edition (PSS Tom 35), reconstructs the eight-year composition from the 1896–1904 diaries and letters, and follows it into its posthumous, censored print.

A few things stood out.

**The seed is an image, and it is exactly datable.** On 18 July 1896, walking near Pirogovo, Tolstoy saw a crushed-but-still-standing "Tatar" thistle by the road. His diary the next day: "It reminded me of Hadji Murat. I want to write it. It defends its life to the last." The thistle becomes the work's first image and its last simile — Hadji Murat falls in his final stand "like a thistle that has been mown down."

**He built it from an archive — unusual for his fiction.** The dive's heaviest layer is the source-hunt. Tolstoy wrote to the critic Stasov at the Imperial Public Library, to Grand Duke Nikolai Mikhailovich for the Tsar's own marginal orders, and to a chain of Tiflis archivists and eyewitnesses at Nukha, chasing Caucasus-war histories and the record of Hadji Murat's own testimony. The PSS source list runs to 82 items; the manuscripts to 2166 sheets; the editors count ten redactions.

**He was ashamed of it the whole time.** The diaries carry a steady thread of self-reproach — "self-indulgence and foolishness," "stupid, almost a mental illness," "I write Hadji Murat and feel ashamed the whole time." The sharpest line names the conflict directly: he wrote it "now with eagerness, now with reluctance and shame." He had spent two decades teaching that art was a lesser calling, and was publishing *What Is Art?* (1897–98) in these very years — yet could not stop writing this.

**So: betrayal or fulfilment of his own theory?** Scholars split three ways — fulfilment (Simmons), violation (Herman), private relapse (Wilson). The diaries show something the finished work can't: Tolstoy never reconciled it. He never argued the story satisfied his aesthetics; he lived the contradiction as guilt and refused to publish. And yet the text withholds all moralizing — its judgment is carried by structure (the Tsar's order → a burned village → a severed head, with the nightingales resuming over it), never by statement — which is the closest it comes to obeying *What Is Art?*'s own attack on didacticism. We rendered it as the record shows it: neither a secret repudiation of his ideals nor a tidy fulfilment of them.

**Two rulers in the same ink.** Tolstoy described his design as "two poles of governmental absolutism — Asiatic and European." The novella builds Nicholas I and Imam Shamil as mirrors: the same staged grandeur, the same self-attributed divine sanction, the same casual cruelty. That parallel is his own; the "anti-imperialist masterpiece" label is the mainstream's, and recent scholarship complicates it.

**The censorship is posthumous.** In 1912 the work appeared in two editions at once — a censored Moscow text that cut the Nicholas I chapter from over ten pages to four-and-a-half and excised the burned-aul chapter, and Chertkov's uncensored Berlin edition that restored everything. The censor objected that "Emperor Nicholas I is subjected to impermissible, extremely crude attacks insulting to his memory." This is the state censoring a dead author over its own founding dynasty.
