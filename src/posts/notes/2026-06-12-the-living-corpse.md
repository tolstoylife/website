*The Living Corpse* (Живой труп) is the one mature work of art Tolstoy wrote and then deliberately buried. Fyodor Protasov cannot live within his marriage, but neither can he get an honest divorce — the Orthodox courts granted one only on proof of adultery, which meant perjury, and «не могу спокойно лгать», he says. So he fakes his own drowning, leaving his wife free to marry the honourable Karenin; he becomes a living corpse. A blackmailer exposes him, all three are tried for bigamy, and rather than let the law "bind me to her again," Fedya shoots himself in the courthouse corridor. No one in the play is a villain. The villain is the institution.

The plot was real. Tolstoy's friend the judge N. V. Davydov gave him the court case of the Gimer couple — the same legal-circle channel that had given him *Resurrection*. The two leads are N. S. and E. P. Gimer; the mother is E. A. Simon; the drink-ruined "genius" of the tavern is Tolstoy's own copyist, A. P. Ivanov — the same Ivanov who, drunk at a Moscow flophouse, leaked the plot to a reporter and helped doom the play.

The dive's two findings turn on the diary. First, a small correction: Tolstoy didn't drift into the play after admiring Chekhov — he was *provoked* by him. «Ездил смотреть Дядю Ваню и возмутился. Захотел написать драму Труп», he wrote on 27 January 1900. Second, the larger one: the standard story says he abandoned the play to spare the real Gimers, who begged him not to publish. That pressure was real. But in his own hand the reason is older and different — on 28 November 1900, after reading an article on the peasants' suffering, «Драму Труп надо бросить»; back in August, «недоволен… Нет сознания, что это — дело Божие»; to Chertkov in December, the play was something written «балуясь» that would only «поощряло бы меня писать легкомысленное». The author of *What Is Art?* was turning against his own fiction, and the play was the casualty. And against the common reading that this drama reverses *The Kreutzer Sonata*, Tolstoy himself filed it with *Kreutzer*, *The Power of Darkness*, and *Resurrection* — works he wrote «без всякой думы о проповеди» that did good anyway. «Не то ли и с Трупом?»

He never finished it. He told Koni in 1904: «оно не кончено… и я его совсем бросил». Then, within a year of his death, the public overturned the verdict twice — in print (*Русское слово*, September 1911) and on the stage. It opened at the Moscow Art Theatre that same month, directed by Nemirovich-Danchenko, with Stanislavski himself playing Prince Abrezkov and Ivan Moskvin as Fedya; it travelled to Berlin, Vienna, Paris, and London, reached Broadway as *Redemption* with John Barrymore, and has been filmed some seventeen times. The play he thought too frivolous to finish became one of the most-staged Russian dramas of the century.
