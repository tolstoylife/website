In December 1908 Tolstoy finished an open letter to Tarak Nath Das, an émigré Indian revolutionary who had asked him to write something on India's subjection to Britain. Tolstoy's answer was not the endorsement Das wanted. India, he argued, is not held down by English force from outside — "thirty thousand people... have enslaved 200 millions" only because the 200 millions accept violence as a principle and keep manning the army, the courts, the tax-office that bind them. The lever of liberation is therefore not resistance but withdrawal: stop participating in the machinery of your own subjection, and the subjection ends.

The dive reads the letter against the diaries that record its writing, and the surprise is how little Tolstoy thought of it. Through the whole composition he called it "weak," "all repetitions," "rubbish and repetition." 413 manuscript sheets lie behind a short letter he mostly wanted to be rid of.

Then it reached Gandhi. Gandhi read it in London in 1909, asked permission to reprint it, and published it in *Indian Opinion* with his own preface — translating it into Gujarati on the same sea voyage he wrote *Hind Swaraj*. The 1909–1910 correspondence that followed runs to Tolstoy's last long letter, eight weeks before his death, calling Gandhi's South African campaign "the most central, the most important of all the affairs being done now in the world." And in March 1910, reading Gandhi's printed version, Tolstoy reversed his verdict: he "very much approved" the letter he had dismissed.

Two complications keep the story honest. Gandhi's satyagraha — organised, mass, confrontational — was not Tolstoy's individual, anarchist withdrawal; the lever Gandhi pulled was not quite the one Tolstoy described. And the man the letter was actually written to, Tarak Nath Das, rejected it: he published an "Open Letter" in reply insisting that "resistance to despotism is the first of all human duties." The letter that shaped Gandhi failed with its addressee.
