If *What I Believe* is where Tolstoy sets the Gospel against the army and the courts, *What Then Must We Do?* (Так что же нам делать?) is where he sets it against money and property. It begins not with doctrine but with a street: in 1881 Tolstoy moved to Moscow, and the city poverty astonished him. In January 1882 he volunteered for the Moscow census, was sent into the Rzhanov-house slum and the Lyapinsky night-shelter, and tried to turn the count into an act of organized charity. The plan failed, he came away ashamed, and the book grew out of that shame. The corpus shows three things worth pulling out.

**Charity is impossible, because the money is stolen to begin with.** Comparing his own twenty-kopeck gift to a beggar with a peasant's gift of three kopecks from a purse of six hundred, Tolstoy works out by arithmetic that his money is not his labour but a draft on other people's — so giving it back as charity only reproduces the poverty it relieves. He states it in the image the book is remembered by: *"I sit on a man's back, choking him and making him carry me, and yet assure myself and others that I am very sorry for him and wish to ease his lot by every possible means — except by getting off his back."* From there he turns on money itself, arguing that it is a bloodless modern form of slavery — no more neutral than serfdom — because it rests on the state's power to demand it by force, and that the «scientific» theory of the division of labour is just the old priestly excuse for «захват чужого труда», the seizure of others' work, dressed in new clothes.

**The answer comes from a peasant, not the wise.** The book's positive doctrine is bread-labour: everyone must do physical work to earn their own bread. Tolstoy credits it, by name and explicitly against the learned world, to the Siberian exile peasant T. M. Bondarev — *"I mentioned that I drew this not from the learned and wise of this world, but from the peasant T. M. Bondarev"* (to Bondarev, March 1886) — and to the sectarian V. K. Sutaev; Henry George's *Progress and Poverty*, read in early 1885, reinforced it. The diaries catch him working it out in private, in an 1884 plan to hand the estate's land to the peasants and teach his children manual labour. The famous final chapter extends the law of labour to women as a law of childbearing, and assigns the salvation of the world to self-sacrificing mothers — the book's hardest turn for a modern reader, and one the dive presents plainly rather than smoothing over.

**It was never cleanly banned — it was refused, cut and dispersed.** Begun in 1882 (not 1884, as the lifetime editions dated it) and written stop-start through 1886, the book was refused serialization by the censor at *Русская мысль*, then passed only in heavily cut fragments in *Русское богатство*, and finally printed whole — but cut — in the collected works of 1886, after Pobedonostsev read the proofs and told Tolstoy's wife there was *"no hope whatever that they will be passed… a book that will have a harmful effect on minds."* The uncensored text appeared abroad (Geneva, 1886; the complete text only in 1902). One thread the dive draws out complicates the familiar story of a wife opposed to her husband's renunciation: S. A. Tolstaya objected to the chapter that named her family critically — not to the book's argument — and got those passages rewritten, while she was at the same time preparing the edition that carried the book and petitioning the censors to let it through.

(Так что же нам делать?)](../../../../docs/research/1882-1886-what-then-must-we-do/index.html). Every Russian quotation is checked byte-for-byte against its source (21/21). As with *A Confession* and *What I Believe*, the work has no record yet in the catalogue; the dive carries a source-grounded proposal to create one.
