When Tolstoy re-translated and harmonized the four Gospels around 1880, he reached the one passage almost every reader already knows by heart — the Lord's Prayer — and rewrote it. This dive pulls that single passage out of the larger work to see exactly what his method did, word by word, and then renders the result into Swedish, under the name the prayer carries here: **«Fader vår»**.

The first surprise is that there is no single new translation. Tom 24 holds a whole family of them: a close retranslation in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew), a transformed version in the chapter "On Prayer" (Luke), a twelve-fold phrase-by-phrase expansion in the preface of *The Gospel in Brief*, a recitable prayer in that book's body, and the French *Abrégé*. They agree in substance and differ in wording. The second surprise is sharper: Tolstoy read the model prayer as the *abolition* of prayer. Since the Father "knows what you need before you open your lips," the prayer is not a formula to repeat but an instruction to stop asking — *"How could it be said more plainly that one must not pray?"*

His signature moves are visible in miniature. "Give us our daily bread" becomes *the nourishment of the spirit*. In Luke he adopts a genuine ancient variant — not "thy kingdom come" but *"may thy spirit descend into us and cleanse us"* — a reading attested in a couple of medieval minuscules, in Marcion, and in Gregory of Nyssa; Tolstoy's footnote shows he knew it was old. And the doxology that ends "the kingdom, the power and the **glory**" becomes "the power, the strength and the **reason**" — glory traded for *разум*, the keyword of his whole gospel.

The diaries and letters of the composition years react to the *labour*, not to the prayer: he names the Sermon on the Mount as "the essence of the teaching," thanks Strakhov for the Tischendorf Greek New Testament (the very edition he cites in his Luke footnote), and reports in July 1881 that the big harmony is finished and the short book made. The prayer itself is worked out entirely inside the text.

The Swedish rendering uses the traditional *Fader vår, som är i himmelen* as its scaffold, so Tolstoy's meaning falls in the cadence already in the ear. Tolstoy's recitable version, in Swedish:

> Bed allenast så: Fader vår, utan begynnelse och utan ände, såsom himmelen!
> Må allenast ditt väsen vara heligt.
> Må allenast makten vara din, så att din vilja sker utan begynnelse och utan ände på jorden.
> Giv mig livets föda i det närvarande.
> Mina forna misstag, utplåna och stryk ut dem, såsom ock jag utplånar och stryker ut alla mina bröders misstag, så att jag icke faller i frestelse, utan varder frälst ifrån ondo.
> Ty din är makten och kraften, och ditt är avgörandet.
