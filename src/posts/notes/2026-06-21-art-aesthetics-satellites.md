*What Is Art?* (1897–98) reads in the West almost as a freestanding manifesto. Four short pieces show it instead as the middle of a working argument, the doctrine tested case by case.

The **preface to the works of Maupassant** (1893–94) turns out to be the treatise's seedbed: three years early it already states the "three conditions of true artistic creation" — a moral relation to the subject, clarity, sincerity — and the PSS editors call it an introductory part of the treatise. Maupassant is the hard case the doctrine was built to handle: a writer Tolstoy grants talent, clarity, and full sincerity, and still rules "bad art" for lacking the moral relation. The **preface to the English edition of What Is Art?** (1898) is stranger — Tolstoy turns the treatise's own test on the treatise's own text, because every Russian edition in his lifetime was mutilated by the censors (the church censor even replaced "Christ went to the cross for the truth" with "for the human race"). A book drained of its «цельность и искренность», wholeness and sincerity, cannot faithfully transmit; the authentic vessel becomes Aylmer Maude's uncensored English.

**On Gogol** (an unfinished 1888 note and a published 1909 article) reads Gogol's despised religious turn sympathetically — Tolstoy clearly recognising a self-portrait in the "reborn" writer the critics called a madman — but the test stays discriminating: it rescues the religious feeling of the letters while still judging the moralising fiction "terrible, repulsive nonsense." And the **preface to Edward Carpenter's "Modern Science"** (1898) is the deliberate science-twin of the art test, written the same months: true science is the knowledge of how to live, and experimental science has usurped that place, serving the classes that fund it exactly as upper-class art does.
