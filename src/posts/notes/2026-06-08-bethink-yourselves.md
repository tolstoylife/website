*Одумайтесь!* (*Bethink Yourselves!*, 1904) is usually filed as Tolstoy's anti-war tract on the Russo-Japanese War. The corpus shows something more specific: it is two essays welded into one, written at speed during a war he could not stop, and published abroad in translation because it could not be printed at home.

**Two themes, joined under pressure.** Tolstoy was already writing a tract on the absence of religion as the root of all human ills when the war broke out on 27 January 1904 (Old Style). His diary that very day asks the question the whole essay would be built on — not what war means for the world, but "how must I, I, I relate to the war?" He fused the two themes and judged the join imperfect, telling Chertkov on 28 April: "Because of this I joined two themes together, and perhaps neither is worked out sufficiently." The war is the surface; the loss of religion is the diagnosis underneath.

**Its method is to replace the collective question with the personal one.** Soldier, officer, diplomat, journalist, and tsar are each asked why they prepare to kill, and each answers with an abstraction — faith, fatherland, oath, civilization. The cure Tolstoy offers is for each to stop and ask "who am I? what is my purpose?" This is the "bethinking" of the title: the Greek μετανοεῖτε of the Gospel, which the translators rendered *bethink yourselves* rather than the narrower *repent*. The remedy is the conscience of one person at a time refusing — not peace conferences, courts, disarmament, or revolution.

**Banned at home, so the translation lineage is the publication history.** The essay first reached the public through Chertkov: in *The Times* (London, 27 June 1904) and as a Free Age Press pamphlet at Christchurch. In Russia the 1906 and 1911 printings were both confiscated; it was never legally published there in Tolstoy's lifetime. The authorised English was Chertkov's, with a co-translator signed "I. F. M." — Isabella Fyvie Mayo, a Scottish writer and suffragist. Swedish, German, and two French versions followed within the year. And Chertkov was editor as much as translator: Tolstoy handed him a "carte blanche," and in proof Chertkov made six documented cuts, all of which Tolstoy approved.

**The Swedish edition quietly rewrites the last sentence.** The 1904 Stockholm edition, *Betänken Eder!* (Björck & Börjesson) — the subject of a parallel digital-edition project, and translated from the Russian with the epigraphs dropped — diverges at the coda. Where the Russian ends "let us not resist it, but serve it" (the fire of Luke 12:49), the Swedish reads "låtom oss då icke släcka den, utan sprida och underhålla den" — "let us not extinguish it, but spread and sustain it." A free rendering, not a different source; but it shifts the verb of the essay's last act from non-resistance toward active propagation.

**Tolstoy's own verdict was split but firm.** A reader reproached him for the essay's anger, and he agreed about the tone while standing by the substance without reservation: "I am at fault that the tone, the spirit of my article is unkind, but its meaning is, for me, undoubtedly true, and I will repeat the same on my deathbed" (1 July 1904). The diaries catch the same split a few weeks earlier — doubt that he had only "stirred up enmity," giving way, at the sight of the conscripts' wives, to regret that he had written "too little, too weakly."
