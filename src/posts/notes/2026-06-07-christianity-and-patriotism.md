In October 1893 a Russian naval squadron under Vice-Admiral Avelan was received at Toulon, and the French and Russian press worked itself into a fortnight of mutual love. Tolstoy watched the festivities, felt "first the comic, then bewilderment, then indignation," and sat down to write what he meant to be a short journal article. It took him six months, and it became *Christianity and Patriotism* (*Христианство и патриотизм*).

This corpus dive reads the essay from its own text (PSS Tom 39) and reconstructs how it was written from the 1893–94 diary and letters. The argument is unusual for a polemic: Tolstoy diagnoses mass patriotism rather than denouncing it. He borrows, by name, the Kiev psychiatrist Sikorsky's term for a peasant religious mania — *malevanshchina*, a "psychopathic epidemic" — and says the Toulon enthusiasm is the same disorder, differing only in that its sufferers "possess rifles, bayonets, fortresses, ironclads, melinite, dynamite" and the press to spread the contagion. Patriotism, on his account, is not natural to ordinary people but manufactured from above by governments, schools and clergy; the proof is that he had "lived half a century among the Russian people and not once seen or heard" it unprompted. Hence the verdict — "patriotism is slavery" — and the remedy, which is not political but personal: "one need only not lie."

The genesis is a study in ambivalence. The diary and his letters to Chertkov record him finishing a draft, declaring it "repugnant," hearing an "inner voice" against publishing, disowning that voice two days later as "very foolish," "pounding water in the Toulon mortar" all winter, deciding to suppress the finished essay — "and that relieved me" — and then, a month later, sending it to the translators after all: "Everyone approves."

Banned in Russia, it appeared abroad first — in French (May 1894), then English and German — and circulated at home in underground hectographs until the first legal Russian printing in 1906, for which the publisher was prosecuted. It is the first of Tolstoy's three escalating anti-patriotism statements and the bridge from the religious treatises of the 1880s to the anti-war essays of the 1900s.
