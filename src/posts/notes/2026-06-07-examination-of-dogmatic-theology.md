Before Tolstoy could write his own Gospel, he had to clear away the Church's. *Examination of Dogmatic Theology* (Исследование догматического богословия) is where he does it: the second, demolishing panel of the four-part religious project he began as a single treatise in late 1879 — *A Confession* → this work → the Gospel harmony → *What I Believe*. It is the most sustained and savage attack he ever made on Orthodox doctrine, and almost no one reads it. The corpus turns up three things worth pulling out.

**He learned the theology "like a good seminarian" in order to destroy it.** Tolstoy took Metropolitan Makary (Bulgakov)'s *Православно-догматическое богословие* — the standard textbook — and went through it dogma by dogma: the Trinity, the divine attributes, creation and the Fall, original sin, the divinity of Christ, redemption, the Church, the sacraments. His charge is not that the theology is mistaken but that it is dishonest: he found in it *"not only senselessness, but a conscious lie of people who had chosen faith as a means of attaining some ends of their own."* The moral heart of the book is one substitution he will not forgive — that the dogma of original sin replaces the living question *how shall I live?* with the fantastical one *why am I bad?* By the chapter on relics and hell, the act of copying the doctrine out has become, for him, the real blasphemy.

**The famous title isn't his.** Every printed edition — and Leo Wiener's 1904 English — calls it *A Critique of Dogmatic Theology* («Критика…»). But that title came from M. Elpidine's first edition (Geneva, 1891); Tolstoy's own autograph reads *Исследование* — examination, investigation — the word he uses in the book's first line and throughout, and the one the Jubilee editors restored. The genesis is thin to match: the corpus has **no diary at all for 1879 or 1880**, so the one firm dateable witness is a letter to N. N. Strakhov of 29 February 1880 — *"I have studied theology thoroughly, and now I am finishing its analysis … If I had been told what I found there, I would not have believed it."*

**It was a book Russia could not print.** Unlike the works that were submitted and then suppressed, this one went straight abroad — Elpidine in Geneva, then Chertkov's *Свободное слово* press at Christchurch in 1903 — and circulated inside Russia in manuscript copies. When it finally appeared legally after 1905, it was censored: the conclusion's comparison of Catherine the Great to *"the orthodox, pious harlot"* and Peter the Great to *"the most pious robber and murderer … who blasphemed against the Gospel"* was cut to dots. And yet the book does not end in pure negation: its last words turn from demolition to Tolstoy's own faith — *"The business of faith is only life according to faith"* — the seed of everything the four-part project went on to build.
