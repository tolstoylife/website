In the autumn of 1903 Tolstoy sat down to write a short preface to an American friend's essay on Shakespeare. He could not stop. What came out was *On Shakespeare and the Drama* (*О Шекспире и о драме*) — a fifty-six-page demolition of the most celebrated name in European literature, built around a deadpan, scene-by-scene retelling of *King Lear* designed to make the play look absurd.

The new corpus dive reads the essay as the **practical sequel to *What Is Art?*** It is the place where Tolstoy takes his theory of art — that the real test is *sincerity*, the genuine "infection" of one person's feeling into another — and runs the single most famous writer in the world through it. Shakespeare, he concludes, fails: he "does not believe what he says." The essay's boldest claim is that Shakespeare's worldwide fame is not earned but is a kind of mass spell — "one of those epidemic suggestions" like the medieval Crusades or the Dutch tulip mania, magnified by the press, with the Dreyfus affair as the lived example.

The dive pins the genesis from the diaries and letters (begun ~13 September 1903, "finished" that December, still being corrected in January 1904), traces the cut drafts — including a striking passage, dropped from the final text, that explains *why* even gifted readers are taken in — and maps the publication and the long, mostly hostile scholarly afterlife, from George Orwell's famous 1947 reply onward. A marquee section tests whether the essay *confirms* or *over-extends* the art doctrine; the honest answer is both, with one joint where it over-reaches.
