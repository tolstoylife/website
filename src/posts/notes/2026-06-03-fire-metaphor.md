Fire is being considered as an emblem for this site, so it was worth checking what Tolstoy actually did with the image. The answer is more pointed than "he liked fire metaphors."

Two things hold together. First, fire is the figure Tolstoy used for the one thing he thought Christ brought: the kindling of conscience. Luke 12:49 — *"I came to cast fire on the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled"* — is his single most-recurrent fire-anchor, running from the Gospel harmony through *What I Believe*, *The Kingdom of God Is Within You*, *Bethink Yourselves!* (1904), his last book *Path of Life* (1910), and at least five letters. He reads it not as judgment but as a spark: *"his teaching was not a doctrine but a spark that kindles the consciousness of God in people's hearts and, once lit, cannot go out."* In the letters the ethic is plain — *"kindle the fire in yourself; burn yourself, and those around you will catch of their own accord"* — and his image for the movement is scattered fires on a dry steppe.

Second, and this is the part to know before adopting fire as a mark: when Tolstoy translated the four Gospels himself, he was *removing* one fire while magnifying the other. The fire of judgment — Gehenna, "eternal fire," "unquenchable fire," the furnace — he excised or naturalised (the "eternal fire" of Matthew 25 becomes "outer darkness"; Gehenna becomes a historical valley). The light, meanwhile, he redefined by rule: *"light, in all contexts, means the true understanding of life"* — so the Logos of John 1 becomes "reason," and the personal, incarnate Light is depersonalised.

The whole argument is caught in one sentence — the credo that closes *What I Believe*, the same passage Biryukov chose to end volume II of his biography: faith is *"a fire which is only a fire when it burns,"* and the task is *"to live by the light that is in me, and set it not under a bushel."* Fire and light, fused, in Tolstoy's own confession.
