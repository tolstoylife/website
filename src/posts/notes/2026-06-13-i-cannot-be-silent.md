In May 1908 Tolstoy read a newspaper report that twenty peasants had been hanged at Kherson for raiding a landowner's estate. He tried to dictate his response into a phonograph and was too shaken to finish. Two days later he wrote the first draft of *Не могу молчать* — *I Cannot Be Silent* — his essay against capital punishment.

This corpus dive reads the essay from its own text (PSS Tom 37) and reconstructs how it was written from the 1908 diary and B. M. Eikhenbaum's editorial history. A few things stand out.

The argument's centre is **personal complicity**. Tolstoy reasons that because the executions are carried out "in the name of the common good," they are carried out for him — his comfortable life is secured by them — and so he asks to be hanged alongside the condemned, "so that with my own weight I tighten the soaped noose on my old throat." The title means exactly what it says: he cannot stay silent because silence would be consent.

The **drafting changed who the essay accuses.** The first draft named "the two chief hidden executioners — Pyotr Stolypin and Nikolai Romanov" and asked to be hanged "as the twenty-first." The published text removed every name and generalised the appeal to everyone in the chain of killing — the named version could only be printed in 1917. And the essay we read is a Tolstoy–Chertkov text: Chertkov edited the manuscript in red ink (with Tolstoy's telegraphed approval) and cut the most defiant closing line.

Finally, the **publication was itself the story.** Russian newspapers printed fragments in July 1908 and were all fined; a Sevastopol publisher was arrested for posting the text around town. The complete essay reached Russia only through an illegal press and circulated abroad — appearing, per its émigré publisher, "simultaneously in the newspapers of almost all civilised countries on 15 July 1908." Tolstoy had asked to be prosecuted; the state fined his printers instead.
