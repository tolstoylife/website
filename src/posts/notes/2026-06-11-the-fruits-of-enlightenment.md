*The Fruits of Enlightenment* (Плоды просвещения) is the comic twin of *The Power of Darkness*. Tolstoy began them in the same autumn of 1886, in the same notebook, both sparked by the same friend reading Ostrovsky and Gogol aloud at his bedside. But where the tragedy was written *for* the people, the comedy was written about — and, in the end, by and for — his own class. It came from a spiritualist séance Tolstoy had himself invited to in the mid-1880s, at N. A. Lvov's Moscow flat, and went to as a scoffer: believing in mediums, he said beforehand, was "just the same as believing that if I suck my walking-stick, milk will flow from it." In the first drafts the credulous landowner simply *is* "Lvov."

The plot is a hoax. Three peasants need the landowner's signature to buy land; he refuses, while the household chases séances and breeds borzois. The maid Tanya — an orphan of the peasants' own village, in love with a peasant lad — exploits the master's spiritualism: she persuades him the lad is a "medium," then, hidden under the sofa, fakes the séance phenomena so that the "spirits" command him to sign the deed. He does. The play's funniest scene is the Professor explaining it all as "the energy of mediumism" and a "spiritual ether"; its sharpest is the end, where, shown the fraud, the believer cannot be moved — the girl's trick, he reasons, merely "solicited" the genuine energy — and sweeps out lamenting "how far we still are from Europe." Beneath the farce runs a darker register: a discarded old cook, once chef to the Emperor, now dying of cold and drink; a maid dead in hospital after she "slipped." The "fruits of enlightenment" are superstition, idleness, and a peasantry left landless.

The dive's question was whether *Fruits* is the inverse of *The Power of Darkness*, and the primary record answers in Tolstoy's own hand. He wrote the staged version because his daughter Tatyana got up a holiday theatrical and asked for it; his daughters played the maid and the cook; the play's reprises were a court performance at Tsarskoye Selo. There was no peasant audience — and he knew the irony. "With clear consciences," he wrote to Biryukov on the last day of 1889, "we were doing, in heightened measure, the very thing the comedy ridicules." He was "ashamed the whole time, ashamed of this senseless extravagance amid poverty." Printed uncensored in 1891, the comedy was nonetheless barred from the *public* stage by Alexander III — "unsuitable for the stage, but on amateur theatres it may be permitted" — so that, for a time, a play mocking the idle gentry could be performed only by the gentry. It opened the public stage at the Alexandrinsky in September 1891; months earlier, a young Konstantin Stanislavski had staged it for his amateur society, playing the spiritualist landowner himself, in what he called his first independent production — a step toward the founding of the Moscow Art Theatre.
