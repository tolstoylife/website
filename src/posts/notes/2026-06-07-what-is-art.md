For fifteen years Tolstoy circled a question he could not finish answering, and in 1897 he finally sat down to it: what *is* art, that we should pay for an opera in tens of thousands of working days? *What Is Art?* (Что такое искусство?) is the answer — written across an agonised year, cut from forty-odd chapters to twenty, and disowned by its own author in its first Russian edition. The corpus turns up three things worth pulling out.

**He threw out beauty and replaced it with "infection."** The whole European tradition of aesthetics, Tolstoy argues, defines art by *красота* — beauty — which on inspection means nothing more than *"то, что нам нравится"* (what pleases us). That is no criterion at all. In its place he puts a definition of startling simplicity: art is *"деятельность человеческая, состоящая в том, что один человек … передает другим испытываемые им чувства, а другие люди заражаются этими чувствами"* — a human activity in which one person transmits a felt feeling and others are *infected* by it. Good art is then judged by a second test — whether it carries the highest religious consciousness of the age, the brotherhood of all people — and everything else is a "counterfeit," faked by four tricks: borrowing, imitation, strikingness, and mere interest.

**He turned the criterion on the whole canon — including himself.** By the infection-and-brotherhood test, most of what the educated call great art fails. Wagner's *Ring* becomes the *model* counterfeit; Beethoven's Ninth Symphony is bad art ("Without any doubt," he answers the indignant voices); the French Decadents, much of Shakespeare, Dante and Raphael go the same way. And then, in a footnote, he relegates his own novels: *"свои художественные произведения я причисляю к области дурного искусства"* — I assign my own artistic works to the domain of bad art — excepting only two short stories. (That footnote, which a standard extractor strips, had to be recovered by hand from the source XML; the single most-quoted line in the book lives in the note apparatus.)

**The censor put words in his mouth — so he wrote a preface to expose it.** Tolstoy meant to publish abroad, but the journal editor N. Ya. Grot talked him into the Russian *Вопросы философии и психологии* and promised to slip it past the censor. First Grot softened the wording (всегда→иногда, церковное→католическое, патриотизм→лжепатриотизм). Then the *spiritual* censor cut whole passages and, where Tolstoy wrote of Christ going to the cross "for the truth he professed," substituted "for the human race" — fastening onto him the redemption dogma he rejected. The preface he wrote for Aylmer Maude's English edition — the first complete, uncensored text (1898) — tells the whole episode as a moral exhibit: *"всякий компромисс с учреждением, не согласным с вашей совестью … неизбежно затягивает вас … в участие в том вреде, который производит это учреждение."* Any compromise with an institution against your conscience drags you into its harm. (The work's catalogue stub marks it as never published in Russia in his lifetime; that is wrong — it appeared, in censored form, in 1897–98. The dive carries the correction.)
