## The brief

Tolstoy's turn around 1878–1882 — from the novelist to the religious teacher — is universally called his "crisis" or "conversion". Pavel Birukoff's biography gives it a chapter under exactly that name: in the Swedish edition, Book IV «Kritisk period», chapter 14 «Krisen» (p. 262). But the same page reports Tolstoy denying that any crisis «i egentlig mening» (in the proper sense) ever happened — saying instead that he had *always* sought life's meaning, and that the change was a «mäktig eruption» (mighty eruption) of forces that had always been in him. The project has already flagged this pattern once, for the label *"Tolstoyanism"* — a word Tolstoy disliked, applied to him from outside. The question for this dive: is "crisis" his word, or theirs?

## What the corpus says

In *A Confession* (*Исповедь*, 1879–1882) — the work where Tolstoy tells the story himself — the word **кризис never appears**, and neither does **перелом** ("turning-point"). At the pivot of the narrative he uses **переворот** (an upheaval), and he pointedly denies a sudden rupture (PSS Tom 23, p. 40):

> Я жил так года два, и со мной случился переворот, который давно готовился во мне и задатки которого всегда были во мне.

> *I lived like that for a couple of years, and there occurred in me an upheaval that had long been preparing within me, and whose seeds had always been in me.* (working English)

Six pages later he says he "could not say when and how this upheaval took place", and that the force of life which returned to him was "not a new one, but the very oldest". His image for the bad years is **остановка жизни** — life coming to a stop — not "crisis". The companion work *What I Believe* tells the same event with another verb again: his life "вдруг переменилась" (suddenly changed).

When Tolstoy *does* write кризис in his letters, it is never about himself. To Strakhov (1894) it is medical — the turning-point of a dying friend's pneumonia. To Annenkova (1894) it is plural and universal — "these crises" are the spiritual "ages" everyone passes through, nothing to take fright at. His word for his own change, in that same letter, is **родился вновь** — "born anew".

The singular "crisis of Tolstoy" turns out to live in the **editorial apparatus** — the Jubilee Edition editors write of his having "lived through the crisis of his views" — and in **biography**, in Birukoff's chapter title. It is the commentators' word, not the subject's.

## What the scholars say

The reference works and popular biographies almost all use the word this dive questions: Britannica files the period under "Conversion and religious beliefs", and biographers from Aylmer Maude ("Nearing the Crisis", 1908) to A. N. Wilson ("religious conversion", 1988) keep it. But specialist scholarship has argued for decades what the corpus shows — that the change was gradual, not a sudden rupture. Richard Gustafson holds that "the man is not two, but one" (1986); Inessa Medzhibovskaya calls it "a long conversion" (2008). What none of them does is notice the word itself: that Tolstoy's own term was *переворот*, and that *кризис* never appears in *A Confession*, is what this dive adds to the older argument. His daughter Tatyana, for her part, recorded that he called the change his "second birth" — close to the «родился вновь» above.
