*The Forged Coupon* (Фальшивый купон) is the most schematic thing Tolstoy ever built. A schoolboy, refused a small sum by his irritated father, alters a 2½-rouble coupon. In Part I that single forgery passes outward from hand to hand — a cheated shopkeeper, a framed and murdered peasant, a robber who learns to kill cleanly — until it reaches a poor widow, Maria Semyonovna, who will not resist her killer and pities him as he cuts her throat. Her death stops the chain. In Part II good runs back through the same kind of chain: the murderer, broken by her, converts in prison and remakes the very men who had been the chain's links.

The dive's surprise is how early and how deliberately Tolstoy designed this. He drew the whole shape in a dream-programme of **1886** — a "bright youth" showing a dreamer the entire history of a forged coupon, and the rule that evil spreads but does not conquer good, while good spreads and does. He stated the mechanism in his diary in **1898**, as physics: evil travels "like the transmission of motion by elastic balls" and is stopped only by "the force that absorbs it" — which is non-resistance. And when he finally wrote it, in a hard push over the winter of 1903–04, he first made the spreading evil literal — little devils leaping from shoulder to shoulder — and then cut every one of them, leaving what he called a "new form, very *sober*."

Published posthumously in 1911, the Moscow edition appeared with its sharpest anti-clerical and pro-sectarian passages cut by the censor; the simultaneous Berlin edition carried them whole. And its most famous afterlife — Robert Bresson's film *L'Argent* (1983), and again Aku Louhimies's *Frozen Land* (2005) — keeps only Part I, the chain of evil, and stops exactly where the redemption would begin. The half the films discard is the half the whole design exists to reach.
