Tolstoy's «После бала» (After the Ball) is taught as a tidy lesson in contrast: a glittering ball, then a dawn flogging, proving the hypocrisy of tsarist society. A dive through the primary record — PSS Tom 34, the 1903 diary and letters — shows a stranger, harder story underneath.

It began with the Kishinev pogrom. The same request from Sholom Aleichem that produced the [1903 folk tales](../../../docs/research/1903-folk-tales/index.html) produced this one too — but where the legends hid their politics in parable, this story put the state's violence on the page directly, and so stayed unprintable in Tolstoy's lifetime. He told Sholom Aleichem he could not say in a legal Russian publication that «одно правительство» — the government alone — was the true culprit. So the story waited; it was first printed only in 1911, after his death.

The plot was an old memory. The whole structure — a colonel who dances a mazurka with his daughter and at dawn flogs a Tatar deserter to death — is already in Tolstoy's 1886 essay «Николай Палкин». The love half is his elder brother Sergei's Kazan courtship of Varvara Koreysh, daughter of the military commander; the dancing father and the flogging officer were, by the Tolstoy museum's account, one real man.

The most striking find is in the variants. The story's verdict was not given but built. The first draft has the narrator submit to the cruelty as something elemental, «like a thunderstorm»; two later drafts have him enter the army and either think it a good thing or become like the colonel and repent only in old age. The published version cuts all of that: the narrator refuses to serve at all — «good for nothing», and the uselessness is the point. Tolstoy wrote his way to non-participation by removing every softer ending.

So the story's own frame — «всё дело в случае», it's all a matter of chance — is a decoy. The real subject is conscience, and the refusal to take part in a world that flogs men by the rules.
