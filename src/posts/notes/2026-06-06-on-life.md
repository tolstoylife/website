After the books on what a Christian must believe and what he must do, *On Life* (О жизни) is where Tolstoy asks what life *is*. It is the philosophical capstone of the Prophet period — the metaphysic under the ethic of *What Then Must We Do?* — and the place where he is most nearly a systematic philosopher, writing for and first reading to a learned society. It began, though, on a sickbed: in the summer of 1886 a leg injury turned to a near-fatal fever, and out of that and a friend's letter about the fear of death the book grew. The corpus turns up three things worth pulling out.

**The argument was in the diary before the letter that supposedly started it.** The standard account — repeated in the Jubilee commentary and the modern critical edition — is that *On Life* began as Tolstoy's reply to a September-1886 letter from Anna Dieterichs on death, written while he lay ill. But the diary of 19 June 1886, before the worst of the illness, already contains the whole book in miniature: *"But reason is in man. It is precisely this that abolishes the contradiction,"* and, at the entry's close, *"Immortal is only that which is not-I. Reason — love — God — nature."* The letter was the occasion; the thought was already there.

**It is a book about reason, not a mystical reverie — and the Church banned it for exactly that.** Tolstoy makes reason (разум) the *law* of human life, as organic law governs a plant; he calls the body the "instrument of life"; and he names Schopenhauer and Hartmann as the pessimists he is *refuting*, not echoing — granting that the self's good is impossible, and answering not with despair but with love, *"the only rational activity of man."* He even anticipated his friends' verdict, writing that they would say *"the dreadful word: mysticism?"* — and rejecting it. So did the censors, from the other side: the Moscow Spiritual Censorship Committee reported the book to the Synod for setting up as the guide of life *"not the word of God, but solely and exclusively human reason."*

**The first edition was printed, then destroyed.** Printed at Mamontov's in 1888 as Part 13 of the collected works — 600 copies, the proofs corrected by the philosopher N. Ya. Grot, who had hosted Tolstoy's 1887 lecture «Понятие жизни» and said he had "called the work to life" — the book went into the ecclesiastical censorship, where Tolstoy expected it to die: *"The book 'On Life' is still in the spiritual censorship. They will hardly pass it."* On 5 April 1888 the Holy Synod ordered all 600 copies surrendered for destruction. The only complete authorized edition was gone before it could be sold. The work survived in translation (Hapgood's English *Life*, 1888; his wife S. A. Tolstaya's French *De la vie*, 1889), in a partial legal Russian printing in «Неделя» (1889), and abroad.
