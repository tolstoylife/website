Tolstoy's opposition to the death penalty is usually told through one essay — *I Cannot Be Silent* (1908). This dive reads the decade around it.

Six works, from the assassination of King Umberto in 1900 to *Three Days in the Village* in 1910, make one continuous argument: that the death penalty was a newcomer to Russian law and a contradiction of the Christianity its defenders claimed; that the state kills far more than the assassins it condemns, and kills in its citizens' name; that government and revolutionary killing are equally guilty; and that the only answer is the refusal to kill — or to abet killing.

The occasions are exact. *Thou Shalt Not Kill* (1900) answers Umberto's assassination. *To the Tsar and His Assistants* and *The Only Means* (1901) answer the spring repression of students and workers. *Thou Shalt Not Kill Anyone* (1907) was provoked by the jailing of the publisher who reprinted the 1900 essay — and already demands that the government "execute me too," a year before *I Cannot Be Silent* made that the marquee. *Capital Punishment and Christianity* (1909) answers an article by the Prime Minister's brother that used the Gospel to defend the gallows; Tolstoy's reply to him was one word, "shame." *Three Days in the Village* (1910) draws the chain whole — from a starving widow's seized sheep up to the ministers "busy with the hanging of people."

The dive reads each work from its own text, reconstructs the genesis from the diaries and the editorial histories, and proposes six new works records (none exists yet).
