On 20–22 February 1901 the Holy Synod of the Russian Orthodox Church declared Leo Tolstoy outside the Church. The popular memory is of a heretic cast out. The primary record tells a different story.

This dive reads seven works together around that event — four that pull the church down (the *Reply to the Synod's Edict*, *To the Clergy*, the satirical legend *The Restoration of Hell*, and *On Religious Tolerance*) and three that build the faith back (*Religion and Morality*, *How to Read the Gospels*, and *The Teaching of Christ for Children*, the gospel retold for peasant children). Read side by side, they make one point plain: the edict did not cause the break. Tolstoy's diary records the excommunication only weeks later, and the word he reaches for is «странное» — *strange*. The principle behind the break — that the church is a worldly institution, not Christianity — he had set down in 1893. The 1901 act only ratified, belatedly and (he argued) ambiguously, a separation he had already made and explained.

A few things the dive turns up. The break is with the church, not with God: the *Reply* accepts the renunciation and inverts its meaning — "I renounced it not because I rose against the Lord, but… because I wished to serve Him" — and ends on the image that answers every call to return: a bird cannot re-enter the eggshell it left. The Russian word for the 1901 act is *отлучение* — a separation, not the liturgical anathema — a distinction Tolstoy seized on and current scholarship now grants him. And the edict's authorship is contested: the Soviet-era record credits Pobedonostsev with drafting it, but recent archival work (Kolstø, 2022) shows the Synod's own bishops drove it while Pobedonostsev opposed it.
