Tolstoy's third and last novel was finished under a deadline that had nothing to do with art. In July 1898 he reopened a book that had stalled for most of a decade and drove it to completion to fund the Doukhobor emigration to Canada — reversing his own refusal of literary fees to do it. The corpus shows the money moving: the 12 October 1898 contract with «Нива» (1,000 roubles a printer's sheet, 12,000 down), and a letter routing the fees straight to Chertkov for the emigration. The deadline reshaped the novel: its sharpest material — the prison-church satire, the political prisoners, the caricature of the Synod's Over-Procurator — all entered only in the 1898 redactions.

The centrepiece is the communion scene (Part I, chs XXXIX–XL): the Orthodox liturgy described in flat, defamiliarised terms — the chasuble a "brocade sack," the consecration "manipulations," the priest who "ate a piece of the body of God" and then "sucked his moustache" and walked out cheerful — with the city's church-bells, a chapter later, calling the free population "to the same service now performed in the prison." It was the single most heavily censored passage in the book: the 1899 censor cut both chapters wholesale, leaving four words, and renumbered the part to hide the gap. Russian serial readers never saw it; it survived only in Chertkov's complete text abroad.

The dive set out to test a strong prior — that this scene triggered the 1901 excommunication. The honest answer from the record is *complicates*. The Synod's decree indicts Tolstoy broadly (the Trinity, Christ's divinity, all the sacraments), and names the Eucharist as "the last and highest degree" of his guilt — the rhetorical climax of a doctrinal list two decades in the making, not its cause. The corpus's own editor calls the novel the «толчок», the impulse to *publish* an act long since decided. *Resurrection* was the proximate trigger; the substance had been accumulating since the 1880s.
